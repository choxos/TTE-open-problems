Studies

Closing the catalog, one problem a week

The catalog records what is open. This is the attempt to close some of it: one study a week, each aimed at a single entry, taken in priority order.

Scope is target trial emulation only: the specification of an emulated protocol, the g-methods used to estimate the effects of sustained treatment strategies, and the benchmarking of emulations against the trials they emulate. A problem that would be the same problem in any observational analysis is out of scope, because it is not what this catalog is about.

Completed

BEN-02: A binary replication label changes with precision alone, and one operator carries almost all of it

Question. BEN-02 says replication is reported as a binary label that mixes estimand mismatch with random error. Holding the true trial-emulation discrepancy fixed, how much of the label is decided by precision rather than by the discrepancy, and which of the operators in use carries that sensitivity?

Answer. For the operators tested, yes, and it is concentrated in one of them. The sensitivity S is the larger of the two operator-specific values at 0.4915, against a threshold of 0.10 declared before the run, with a 95 percent Monte Carlo interval of 0.4903 to 0.4931; the lower endpoint clears the threshold by roughly 538 Monte Carlo standard errors. Confidence-interval overlap supplies that value; direction agreement moves by 0.1244 over the same range. Two benchmark pairs carrying the same true discrepancy can therefore be labelled agreeing and disagreeing on the strength of how precisely each was estimated, and the operator most used to produce the label is the most exposed.

What it does not answer. S is the maximum over the operators tested, so the result is an at-least-one-operator claim: it establishes that interval overlap is materially precision-sensitive and that direction agreement is less so, not that every binary replication label is. It holds for these two illustrative operators, these 16 fixed scenarios and these three nested precision levels. This is the pair-level component of BEN-02 and not the entry as a whole: it says nothing about between-pair heterogeneity across a benchmarking program, about calibration of a graded metric against a decision, or about the graded alternatives the entry’s own audit established already exist, all three of which the design review named as out of scope and which are declared limitations rather than results. Outcomes are binary at a single horizon, so the behavior of these operators on a time-to-event scale is untouched.

CNF-01: A single cumulative-initiation number at the horizon does not determine the attenuation it is asked to explain

Question. CNF-01 asks authors to publish the cumulative incidence of initiation in both arms so a reader can see how far the contrasted strategies stayed distinct, and to quantify by simulation how much attenuation a given convergence rate produces. Does that diagnostic actually recover the attenuation?

Answer. No. Within the simulated grid, comparator-arm cumulative initiation at 120 months does not uniquely determine attenuation: matched early and late initiation scenarios, whose reported diagnostic agrees to within 0.0005, differ in attenuation by 0.0528 to 0.2027. Nine of the twelve matched pairs exceed the 0.05 threshold declared before the run by at least three Monte Carlo standard errors, at 5 to 46 of them; the other three sit within 3 and are individually inconclusive, and all three are at the lowest convergence rate, where there is least attenuation to misattribute. A single cumulative-incidence value at the horizon is therefore insufficient for recovering attenuation in these scenarios. What the reported number cannot see is when the comparator arm initiated and who initiated.

What it does not answer. The comparison holds the diagnostic fixed only at 120 months. At earlier horizons the early and late scenarios differ in observed cumulative initiation by as much as 0.269, so those contrasts are not comparisons at equal C(h) and cannot independently establish insufficiency there. The study establishes that C(120) alone is insufficient within this grid. It does not establish that reporting C is necessary, that the full initiation curve would be sufficient, or that a universal conversion from convergence to attenuation exists. The comparator arm converges upward and the treated arm never discontinues, so this is one direction of convergence only. There is no loss to follow-up and no competing event, both excluded so the attenuation is not confounded with a second mechanism. The scale is the risk difference throughout; a hazard ratio would mix this mechanism with non-collapsibility.

How a problem gets chosen

Not every open problem can be settled by a study, and the difference is not cosmetic. A simulation can measure bias, coverage and error rates, and it can show how badly a known nonidentification bites at realistic sample sizes. It cannot establish that a package is missing, that clinicians misread a plot, that a field under-reports something, or that a quantity is unidentifiable in principle. Those need software, a case study, a meta-research corpus, or a proof.

Every one of the 43 catalog entries was judged on both questions separately, by an external model, before the program started: is this a target trial emulation problem, and what kind of study would settle it. The queue below is the intersection.

What each study has to do

Simulation studies follow ADEMP: aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods, performance measures (Morris, White and Crowther 2019). Two rules follow from it and are enforced rather than encouraged.

Every performance measure carries a Monte Carlo standard error.
A bias of 0.02 is uninterpretable without knowing whether the Monte Carlo error is 0.001 or 0.03. The replicate count is derived in the protocol from a target Monte Carlo standard error, not chosen because it is a round number.
Failed replicates are results.
A method that converges on 60% of replicates and is unbiased on those is not an unbiased method. Convergence is reported for every method in every scenario.
The protocol is committed before the run.
It fixes the design and states in advance what result would count as showing the problem is real and what would count as showing it is not. The commit that adds it is the timestamp.

Code, protocols and results are at github.com/choxos/TTE-open-problems/tree/main/studies.
