Statement

Eligibility thresholds, comparators, grace periods, washout windows and outcome definitions are routinely revised after investigators inspect the data. Even an investigator who never looks at a treatment effect can be influenced by outcome prevalence, by covariate-outcome associations or by which specification yields a workable sample size. Standard inferential theory conditions on a fixed target trial and takes no account of the search over candidate emulations that produced it, so the reported confidence interval has the wrong coverage for the procedure actually run. A protocol chosen by an algorithm is an estimated object with its own sampling variability, and nothing in current practice treats it as one.

Why it is open

The search is usually undocumented, so even a method that corrected for it would have no record of what was searched. Selective-inference corrections exist for well-defined selection events such as variable selection under a known rule, and protocol development is not a well-defined selection event: it is a sequence of human decisions with no likelihood. Splitting the data into design and analysis halves solves the inference problem and costs precision in exactly the settings, rare treatments and rare outcomes, where precision is already the binding constraint.

Probable solution or research direction

Divide the data into a design partition and an analysis partition, or expose only outcome-blinded metadata during protocol development, so that the search happens where it cannot contaminate inference. Develop selective-inference corrections and specification-curve estimands adapted to the emulation setting, where the selection is over protocols rather than over covariates. Require dated decision logs so that the search becomes a recorded object; a correction is impossible while the search history is unavailable even to the investigators.

Our study

A recorded, effect-blind search over highly correlated protocols leaves naive intervals close to nominal on average, and undercovering when the candidates disagree

The question put to it. PRO-02 says inference conditions on a fixed target trial that was in fact selected after seeing the data. When the search is recorded and the selector is blind to the treatment effect, choosing among 4 or 16 candidate protocols by outcome counts or covariate-outcome associations, how much coverage do naive 95% intervals for the selected protocol lose, and what do Bonferroni correction and sample splitting cost?

What it found. Not materially, within this design. The global deficit D_all was 0.0015 (simultaneous 95% Monte Carlo interval -0.0001 to 0.0030) and D_nonboundary 0.0023 (0.0004 to 0.0042), both upper limits far below the registered 0.02 threshold; all 360 fixed-candidate evaluations passed the calibration gate. The nonboundary deficit is detectably above zero, but it is about a fifth of a percentage point. Averaged over replicates, a recorded, effect-blind choice among these protocols costs naive intervals almost nothing.

Results.

  • The average hides a conditional failure. Naive noncoverage was 0.022 to 0.025 when the candidates’ estimates spanned less than one standard error, 0.041 to 0.044 when they spanned one to two, and 0.082 to 0.083 when they spanned more than two. The spread of the candidate estimates predicts noncoverage with an AUC of 0.66 to 0.68, so a reader who sees a wide multiverse has reason to distrust the selected interval even though coverage is nominal on average.
  • The design can see a coverage loss when there is one. Selecting the protocol with the largest effect estimate, the registered positive control, lowered naive coverage to 0.922 on average under the null and to 0.894 to 0.928 across the 16-candidate cells, against 0.946 to 0.951 for the effect-blind selectors. Even deliberate effect seeking loses only two to five percentage points here, because the candidates are so highly correlated.
  • The libraries were nearly one protocol. Nested age thresholds and outcome horizons produce highly correlated candidates: the effective library size was 1.37 to 1.39 for 4 candidates and 1.76 to 1.85 for 16. Little is being searched over, which is why the average deficit is small. Libraries of less correlated protocols are untested.
  • Both remedies worked and both cost precision. Selected-protocol Bonferroni intervals covered 0.9915, overshooting nominal; sample splitting covered 0.947 and gave intervals a median 1.42 times as wide as the full-sample interval for the same candidate, close to the square root of two expected from halving the data.
  • The first run’s gate failure was chance. It required every one of 360 coverage estimates to lie in [0.93, 0.97] and one came in at 0.9285, 0.26 Monte Carlo standard errors outside. The replication, drawn from a new seed under a gate registered before it ran, passed all 360, with coverage 0.929 to 0.961.

Design.

Estimand
Unconditional coverage of the naive 95% interval for the selected protocol’s risk difference, as the deficit D = 0.95 minus coverage, globally (D_all) and outside the near-boundary event-count regime (D_nonboundary)
Methods compared
naive interval after workability, prognostic-association or outcome-blinded selection, naive interval after effect-seeking selection (positive control), Bonferroni over the declared library, fifty-fifty sample splitting
Data-generating mechanism
36 generated-data cells crossing cohort size (4000, 8000), expected pooled events at the anchor protocol (age at least 55, 36 months: 35, 70 or 140), library size (4 or 16 candidates over nested age thresholds and outcome horizons) and treatment effect (null, hazard ratio 0.75, age-modified), 2000 replicates each. Four selectors that never see the treatment effect: a workability rule on arm sizes and pooled event counts, two prognostic-association rules differing in how closely their fixed score tracks the outcome, and an outcome-blinded covariate-balance rule; an effect-seeking selector is the positive control. Hajek inverse probability weighted risk differences with a correctly specified propensity model. Truth by numerical integration to a Monte Carlo standard error of at most 0.0000166. A first run with the same design failed its registered calibration gate by chance and was recorded as inconclusive; this is the separately registered replication with a new seed and a gate that allows for Monte Carlo error across the 360 fixed-candidate evaluations.
Replicates
2000 per cell, 36 cells, 1,728,000 rows, 0 replicate errors

What this does not answer. The conclusion covers deterministic recorded searches over nested age thresholds and outcome horizons, libraries of 4 or 16 highly correlated candidates, treatment-effect-blind selectors, and correctly specified inverse probability weighting. It does not cover undocumented or sequential protocol development by an analyst who has seen results, other protocol components such as grace periods or treatment definitions, libraries of weakly correlated protocols, or misspecified weights. The replication’s calibration gate controls false failure across 360 comparisons and in exchange would pass a candidate miscalibrated by less than about two percentage points.

Literature and prior-art check
partially-solved / supported-with-caveat. The entry says standard inference conditions on a fixed target trial and ignores the search over candidate emulations. The search is now being reported in at least one applied setting: a 2025 European Journal of Epidemiology study uses a multiverse analysis to characterize uncertainty in the estimated effect of statin initiation on dementia across design choices. That is the design multiverse the entry proposes, already run. What remains unaddressed is the inferential half: a multiverse displays the spread and does not correct coverage, and no selective-inference treatment of protocol selection exists in the corpus. The entry should not claim the multiverse is absent. Cites 10.1007/s10654-025-01231-y: Runs the design multiverse the entry proposes, in an applied emulation, which means the proposal has a precedent rather than being untried.. Cites 10.1093/aje/kwae346: Documents the specification choices oncology emulations actually make, which is the empirical base for describing the search space..
GPT-5.6 Sol, technical and source-code lens
open / overstated. Outcome-dependent selection of a protocol using the analysis data can invalidate an interval calibrated for a protocol fixed in advance, so the narrowed problem is real. It is not true that every data-informed revision produces wrong coverage. Choices based only on treatment and covariate information may be handled conditionally, independent design and analysis samples break the relevant dependence, and simultaneous or selection-aware inference can cover specified searches. An algorithmically selected protocol produces a data-adaptive target; that does not automatically create an extra variance component, because coverage must first be defined conditionally or marginally and for either the selected estimand or a fixed scientific estimand. An undocumented human search precludes an exact reconstruction of that selection event, but it does not make all valid inference impossible. High severity is warranted only when outcome-relevant selection is material and ignored.
Grok 4.5, recency lens
open / supported. Selective-inference and post-selection literature exists for covariates and models, not for human search over TTE protocol components (eligibility, grace period, washout, outcomes). TARGET and Hansford 2025–2026 report the final protocol; they do not correct coverage for data-dependent protocol selection. Outcome-blinded design splits and dated decision logs remain proposals, not standard practice or validated TTE-specific selective-inference procedures. No CRAN/GitHub change for TrialEmulation/lmtp/ltmle/gfoRmula treats the protocol as a random object. Open.
GLM-5.2, third independent lens
open / supported. The claim is internally consistent and proportionate. It asserts that current practice does not treat the protocol as an estimated object with sampling variability. The reason given for it being open—that the search is undocumented and protocol development is not a well-defined selection event—directly supports the claim that standard inferential theory and current practice fail to account for this search. The scope is correctly pitched at current practice.

How the evidence falls in time

39 papers in the reviewed corpus bear on this problem, published 2009 to 2026. Partial progress is on the record, and later work still calls this open. Both belong in view.

This problem is recurrent: three or more papers spanning at least eight years assert it independently. Sustained restatement by authors who mostly do not cite each other is the strongest evidence this reading can offer that a gap is real rather than one group’s framing.

It is also being closed in pieces: partial results come from more than one paper in more than one year, each covering a different part.
